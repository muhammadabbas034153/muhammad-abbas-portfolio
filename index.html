Three Phase AC Voltage Controllers

A comprehensive academic module on controlling RMS voltage for balanced R and RL loads.

What is a Three-Phase AC Voltage Controller?

A Three-Phase AC Voltage Controller (also known as an AC voltage regulator) is a power electronics converter that converts a fixed AC voltage into a variable AC voltage at the same frequency. This is achieved by utilizing back-to-back (anti-parallel) connected Silicon Controlled Rectifiers (SCRs) or TRIACs in each phase.

By controlling the firing angle (α) of these thyristors, the RMS value of the output voltage applied to the load is smoothly controlled from zero to the maximum supply voltage.

► Why it is Used

  • To control the speed of induction motors (stator voltage control).
  • For soft-starting of large AC motors to reduce inrush current.
  • Industrial heating controls (e.g., resistive heating furnaces).
  • Light dimming systems for large-scale facilities.
  • On-load transformer tap changing.

► Controller vs. Rectifier

  • Output: Controller yields variable AC; Rectifier yields DC.
  • Frequency: Controller output frequency equals input frequency; Rectifier has DC with ripple frequency.
  • Topology: Controller uses anti-parallel SCRs per phase; Rectifier uses a bridge arrangement blocking reverse current.
  • Power Flow: Controllers are inherently bidirectional; basic rectifiers are unidirectional.

Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages
  • ✓ High efficiency (negligible conduction losses).
  • ✓ Compact size and solid-state reliability.
  • ✓ Smooth and continuous control.
  • ✓ Less maintenance compared to auto-transformers.
Disadvantages
  • ✗ Introduces significant harmonics into supply and load.
  • ✗ Input power factor is poor at large firing angles.
  • ✗ Cannot change output frequency.

Circuit Configuration

Topology and current paths for Star and Delta connected loads.

Anti-Parallel SCR Arrangement

The core of the three-phase AC voltage controller is the use of two SCRs connected in anti-parallel (back-to-back) in each phase. Let's denote them as T1 & T4 for Phase A, T3 & T6 for Phase B, and T5 & T2 for Phase C.

Phase A Supply ——— [ T1 → | ← T4 ] ——— Load A

Phase B Supply ——— [ T3 → | ← T6 ] ——— Load B

Phase C Supply ——— [ T5 → | ← T2 ] ——— Load C

Odd numbered SCRs (T1, T3, T5) conduct positive half cycles.
Even numbered SCRs (T4, T6, T2) conduct negative half cycles.

Star (Y) vs Delta (Δ) Loads

Star Connected Load

In a star connection without a neutral wire (3-wire system), at least two SCRs in different phases must conduct simultaneously to establish a closed path for current to flow between lines. If only one SCR is gated, no current flows. Phase voltage applied to the load depends on whether 2 or 3 SCRs are conducting.

Delta Connected Load

In a delta connection, the phase load is directly connected across line voltages. Each anti-parallel pair controls the line voltage applied to the phase load. Current can circulate within the delta if not carefully controlled, leading to third harmonic currents being trapped inside the delta loop.

Resistive vs RL Load Behavior

Resistive (R) Load: The current waveform is exactly identical in shape to the voltage waveform. Current instantly drops to zero when voltage crosses zero. Natural commutation occurs exactly at voltage zero-crossings.

Inductive (RL) Load: Inductance opposes changes in current. Therefore, the load current lags the voltage. When the phase voltage crosses zero, the energy stored in the inductor keeps the SCR forward-biased, causing it to conduct beyond the zero-crossing angle (β > π). This limits the control range of the firing angle and requires continuous or wide gate pulses.

Principle of Operation

Phase angle control and the significance of firing sequences.

Phase Angle Control & Natural Commutation

The output voltage is regulated by delaying the firing angle (α) of the SCRs relative to the zero-crossing of their respective phase voltages. Because we are dealing with AC, the SCRs automatically turn off (commutate) when the current falls below the holding current, typically near the voltage zero-crossing. This is known as Line Commutation or Natural Commutation.

The 60° Separation Rule

A complete AC cycle is 360°. In a balanced three-phase system, we use 6 SCRs. To maintain symmetrical output waveforms and eliminate DC components, the firing pulses must be equally spaced. Therefore, the gate pulses are generated sequentially at intervals of:
360° / 6 = 60°

The standard firing sequence is T1 → T2 → T3 → T4 → T5 → T6.
T1 fires at α. T2 fires at α+60°. T3 fires at α+120°, and so on.

Operating Regions (Star-Connected Resistive Load)

The operation of a 3-wire star-connected controller is complex because the neutral point is floating. The potential of the neutral point shifts depending on how many SCRs are conducting. We divide the operation into three distinct regions based on α:

Region I: 0° ≤ α ≤ 60°

In this region, the conduction alternates between 3 SCRs and 2 SCRs.

  • When 3 SCRs conduct: The load neutral point connects to the supply neutral. The phase voltage across the load equals the supply phase voltage (e.g., Van_load = Van_supply).
  • When 2 SCRs conduct: One phase is open. The line voltage divides equally across the two conducting phases (e.g., Van_load = 0.5 * Vab).
  • The transition happens continuously. For example, T1, T5, T6 might be conducting (3 SCRs). Then T5 turns off naturally, leaving T1 and T6 conducting (2 SCRs) until T2 is fired.

Region II: 60° ≤ α ≤ 90°

In this region, exactly 2 SCRs conduct at any given time.

  • Before the third SCR can be fired, one of the two currently conducting SCRs naturally commutates.
  • Therefore, the load voltage is always half of the corresponding line voltage.
  • The output voltage waveform has no intervals where it equals the phase voltage, and no zero-voltage intervals. It jumps from one line voltage fraction to another.

Region III: 90° ≤ α ≤ 150°

In this region, the current becomes discontinuous. Either 2 SCRs conduct or 0 SCRs conduct.

  • When 2 SCRs are fired, current flows, but it drops to zero before the next SCR is fired.
  • This creates "dead zones" where the load voltage is absolute zero.
  • As α approaches 150°, these dead zones widen until the output voltage becomes completely zero at α = 150°. (For a resistive load, maximum possible α is 150°).

Line Voltage Dominance Concept

In a 3-wire star load, because the neutral is isolated, current can only flow if it can travel out one phase and return through another. Thus, the actual driving force is the Line-to-Line Voltage. When calculating the phase voltage across a resistor in this setup, it is simply half the line voltage of the two conducting phases (e.g., Van = Vab / 2).

Mathematical Derivations

Rigorous RMS and Power equations for Star-Connected Resistive Load.

1. RMS Output Phase Voltage (Vo(rms))

Because the waveform shape changes drastically across the three regions, the integration bounds change. Let Vs be the RMS phase supply voltage.

Region I (0 ≤ α ≤ 60°):
Vo(rms) = Vs √[ 1 - (3α / 2π) + (3 sin(2α) / 4π) ]
Region II (60° ≤ α ≤ 90°):
Vo(rms) = Vs √[ (1/2) + (3√3 / 4π) cos(2α) ]
Region III (90° ≤ α ≤ 150°):
Vo(rms) = Vs √[ (5/4) - (3α / 2π) + (3 sin(2α) / 4π) + (3√3 cos(2α) / 4π) ]

2. RMS Load Current & Power

For a purely resistive load (R) per phase:

RMS Phase Current:
Io(rms) = Vo(rms) / R
Total Three-Phase Real Power Delivered (P):
P = 3 × Vo(rms) × Io(rms) = 3 × Io(rms)2 × R

3. Power Factor Analysis

In phase-controlled circuits with non-sinusoidal currents, the True Power Factor contains both displacement and distortion components.

  • Displacement Factor (DPF): Related to the phase shift of the fundamental current component. DPF = cos(φ1)
  • Distortion Factor (DF): Related to harmonic content. DF = I1(rms) / I(rms)
True Power Factor (PF):
PF = P / S = (3 × Vo(rms)2 / R) / (3 × Vs × Io(rms)) = Vo(rms) / Vs

Important Insight: Even with a purely resistive load, the input power factor decreases as α increases because the chopped waveform introduces reactive and harmonic power drawn from the source.

MATLAB/Simulink Implementation

A guide to designing and analyzing the controller in Simulink.

Required Blocks

  • Source: Three-Phase Programmable Voltage Source
  • Switches: 6x Thyristor blocks (or Universal Bridge set to Thyristors)
  • Control: 6-Pulse Generator (synchronized)
  • Load: Three-Phase Series RLC Branch
  • Measurements: Three-Phase V-I Measurement, RMS block
  • Environment: powergui (Continuous or Discrete)

MATLAB Script: THD Calculation

% MATLAB Code to Calculate and Plot FFT
fs = 10000; % Sampling frequency
t = 0:1/fs:0.1; % Time vector
f = 50; % Fundamental frequency

% Simulated Output Voltage (simplified chopping logic)
alpha = pi/3; % 60 degrees
v_out = 311 * sin(2*pi*f*t); 
v_out(mod(2*pi*f*t, pi) < alpha) = 0; 

% FFT Analysis
L = length(v_out);
Y = fft(v_out);
P2 = abs(Y/L);
P1 = P2(1:L/2+1);
P1(2:end-1) = 2*P1(2:end-1);
freq = fs*(0:(L/2))/L;

% Calculate THD
fundamental = P1(find(freq==f,1));
harmonics_rms = sqrt(sum(P1(freq>f & freq<1000).^2));
THD = (harmonics_rms / fundamental) * 100;

figure; bar(freq(1:50), P1(1:50)); 
title(['Harmonic Spectrum, THD = ', num2str(THD), '%']);
xlabel('f (Hz)'); ylabel('|P1(f)|');

Simulink Pulse Generator Setup

To achieve phase control, use the synchronized 6-pulse generator. Set the frequency to match the source (50Hz/60Hz). The alpha block input takes the firing angle in degrees. Ensure the synchronization phase matches the phase shift of your source (usually Phase A = 0°).

Practical Hardware Implementation

Bringing the theory to a physical printed circuit board.

1. Isolation & Gate Driving

Microcontrollers (e.g., Arduino, DSP) operate at 3.3V/5V, while the AC mains is 230V/400V. Galvanic isolation is strictly mandatory.

  • Use Optocouplers (like MOC3021) for firing signals.
  • Use Pulse Transformers for high dV/dt immunity.

2. Synchronization (ZCD)

To measure α, the controller must know when the AC waveform crosses zero.

  • Zero Crossing Detector (ZCD) circuits use bridge rectifiers and opto-isolators (like 4N25) to send a pulse to the microcontroller interrupt pin exactly at 0°, 180°, 360°.

3. Protection Circuits

  • RC Snubber Networks: Placed in parallel with each SCR to protect against high dV/dt which can cause false triggering.
  • Inductors (Chokes): In series to protect against high dI/dt during turn-on.
  • Fast-acting Fuses: To protect SCRs from short-circuit currents.

4. Thermal Management

Though efficient, SCRs drop ~1.5V when conducting. At 100A, that's 150W of heat per SCR. Large aluminum heat sinks with forced air cooling are required to maintain junction temperatures below 125°C.

Research-Level Insights: Modern Improvements

The traditional phase-controlled thyristor AC controller suffers from poor power factor and high low-order harmonics. Modern research and industrial upgrades focus on:

  • PWM AC Choppers: Using fully controllable switches (IGBTs) instead of SCRs. This allows multiple chopping pulses per half-cycle, pushing harmonics to higher frequencies which are easier to filter, and dramatically improving the power factor.
  • AI-Based Firing Control: Using Neural Networks or Fuzzy Logic controllers to dynamically adjust α in real-time for non-linear varying loads (like arc furnaces) to optimize power quality.
  • Matrix Converters: The ultimate evolution, directly converting AC to AC with variable voltage and frequency without a DC link, replacing standard controllers in high-end applications.

Viva Preparation & Summary

Quick-fire questions and essential takeaways.

Q: Why are gate pulses separated by exactly 60 degrees?
A complete cycle is 360°. In a balanced 3-phase system, we utilize 6 thyristors (2 per phase). To maintain symmetry in the output voltage and ensure no DC offset is injected into the AC supply, the conduction must be evenly distributed. Therefore, 360° / 6 = 60° intervals.
Q: What happens if you use a resistive-inductive (RL) load instead of purely resistive?
The inductor opposes a change in current, causing the load current to lag the voltage. When the phase voltage crosses zero, the stored energy in the inductor keeps the SCR forward-biased, delaying its natural commutation. The current continues to flow for an extinction angle β > π. This limits the control range; you cannot reduce the output voltage effectively if α < load phase angle (φ).
Q: Why does the True Power Factor worsen as the firing angle α increases?
Increasing α chops larger portions of the waveform. This has two effects: 1) It shifts the fundamental component of the current, worsening the displacement factor. 2) It heavily distorts the current from a pure sine wave, drastically increasing the Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) and worsening the distortion factor. Since True PF = Displacement Factor × Distortion Factor, the overall PF drops significantly.

Quick Revision Summary

  • Component: Back-to-back SCRs (6 total for 3-phase).
  • Function: Fixed AC to Variable AC (Constant Frequency).
  • Control Method: Phase Angle Control (α).
  • Commutation: Natural / Line Commutation.
  • Regions (Star, R-Load): I (0-60°), II (60-90°), III (90-150°). Maximum control up to 150°.
  • Major Drawback: High harmonics and poor input power factor at lower output voltages.
